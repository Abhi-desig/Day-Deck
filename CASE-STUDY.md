# Day Deck

**A local-first macOS app that turns big goals into a swipeable deck of day cards — and grew a film studio on the side.**

|---|---|

---

## What Day Deck is

Day Deck is a desktop planner in which your life runs as a deck of day cards you shuffle with a flick. Each card holds the day's real work — tasks decomposed from long-running goals, weekly recurring commitments, reminder notes, and quick-added items — and the whole application is built on the premise that the day, not the week and not the project, is the atomic unit a person can actually act on. Around that spine grew six more rooms: a Goals Admin that turns a pasted syllabus into dated work, an embedded coding workspace with a real terminal, a time tracker derived entirely from what actually got done, a sticker wall for the things that are not tasks, a complete storyboard-to-video studio, and a settings vault that can point every AI feature at a different provider with a different key.

Everything lives in one SQLite file on the user's own machine. There are no accounts, no sync service, and no telemetry. Every feature except generation itself works with the network switched off, and the only outbound calls in the entire application go to whichever AI providers the user has configured — made exclusively from the main process, through a single HTTP module, so that timeouts, retries, rate-limit handling and the question of what a refusal actually means are decided once rather than re-decided per feature.

The interesting part of this project is not any single feature. It is that one person, working conversationally with an AI coding agent, shipped a packaged, test-guarded, multi-surface native application in about ten days without surrendering architectural judgment along the way. The artifacts that made that possible are unglamorous: plan documents written before code, decisions recorded together with the option that was rejected, invariants moved into tests so they would survive the next conversation, and a habit of measuring before optimising — including the discipline to accept a measurement that said the optimisation was not worth doing.

## The problem

Curriculum-style self-teaching dies the same death every time. Somebody converts an ambitious goal into a syllabus, and then nobody converts the syllabus into *what do I do today*. A missed day silently wrecks the schedule downstream, the plan and reality quietly diverge, and the guilt compounds faster than the learning does. The failure is never a lack of ambition; it is the absence of a mechanical, honest bridge between the plan and the calendar.

Almost every AI planner on the market fails in the opposite direction, and just as reliably. It hands you an authoritative-sounding plan and writes it into your calendar before you have finished reading it. The output looks confident, the dates look precise, and the user's only options are to accept the whole thing or throw it away — which means the plan is never actually theirs, and the first time it is wrong they stop trusting it entirely.

Day Deck's founding bet was therefore threefold. First, make the day the atomic unit: everything in the data model resolves to a row on a specific calendar day, and everything the user sees is framed as *today's card*. Second, keep an honest diary of where time actually went rather than a wishful one — which means the record is derived from completions, never from intentions, and a missed day stays visible as a miss. Third, make **every** generative surface in the application ask permission before it writes. Not most of them, and not the expensive ones only: every one, with a single narrowly-argued exception that documents itself in its own file header.

## How this was built

"Vibe-coding" here did not mean prompting and praying. It settled into a repeatable loop, and the loop is the actual substance of this case study.

### Plans before prompts

Every milestone began as a written plan document with an auto-generated codename, committed to the repository alongside the code it described. There are four of them. `day-deck-is-a-iridescent-cookie` is the master plan: four phases, the architecture, a researched animation stack, a motion specification, and a ready-to-paste handoff prompt at the end of each phase so that the next agent session could start from a known state rather than from a summary. `in-the-storyboard-section-merry-cupcake` covered the storyboard canvas and the surrounding milestone. `in-the-story-board-modular-waffle` covered the scene container and the graph model — the move from a canvas of loose rectangles to a single readable list. `four-changes-frosted-doughnut` is the most recent: the locked cast, the style families, the editable prompt desk, and the section-switching lag.

These documents did more than sequence work. Each named its phase's invariants and its single **highest-risk item** before any code existed. For Phase 1 that item was rollover correctness — flagged in the plan as the thing to attack by setting the system clock forward and relaunching repeatedly, which is precisely how it was tested. For the cast phase, the plan opened by stating what "like a LoRA" could *honestly* mean in an application that fine-tunes nothing, and concluded that character consistency is three stacked mechanisms rather than one: the description restated verbatim in every prompt, reference-image conditioning at generation time, and — the load-bearing insight — a pack of angles generated *from the approved portrait* rather than re-prompted from the description. That third point was written down specifically because it is the one part of the feature that is easy to build wrongly and impossible to spot afterwards: a pack of five plausible faces looks exactly like a pack of five shots of one face until you put two of them side by side.

The animation stack was researched rather than picked. The plan surveyed the component libraries and concluded that every animated-component library worth using in 2026 is a Tailwind skin over the same underlying engine, so the decision was to install `motion` once and then cherry-pick MIT-licensed components from Motion Primitives, Magic UI and Aceternity, reimplementing each against the project's own design tokens so nothing could drift. Aceternity got an explicit warning in the plan — its installer writes a Tailwind **v3** configuration, which would have quietly broken a v4 project — so its components were copied by hand and ported.

Not everything planned was built, and that is recorded too. The `merry-cupcake` milestone opened with a Slack mention inbox: a migration, a secret, a client, a poller, an IPC surface and a panel. It was scoped, specified in detail, and then dropped in favour of the storyboard work. There is no `services/slack.ts` in the repository and no half-built table in the schema. A plan is a proposal, not a commitment, and the cheapest place to cancel a feature is in the document that describes it.

### Decisions with receipts

Forks in the road were resolved as explicit either/or choices, and the rejected option was recorded alongside the chosen one — in the plan documents at the time, and then permanently in the code as file-header comments that explain not just what a module does but what it refused to do. `services/secrets.ts` explains why provider configuration lives in the settings table while the key itself does not. `services/ffmpeg.ts` opens by stating the two traps it exists to avoid before it defines a single function. `shared/processes.ts` spends its first twenty lines explaining why a prompt cannot be one editable string. The effect is that nine months of "wait, why did we do it this way?" is pre-answered inside the file where the question will be asked.

### One principle, for the product and the workflow

**AI proposes, you approve.** Inside the application, nothing AI-generated reaches the database without an explicit apply step: plan previews, scene breakdowns, prompt rewrites, revision requests, cut proposals, and style field-sorting all wait for a click, and the split between reading and writing lives in the *service* rather than in the dialog, so the guarantee is a property of the API rather than a promise the UI is making on its own behalf. In the build, the same rule governed the collaboration: the agent drafted, the human reviewed, and every commit and push was run by hand. That was not a statement about trust. It was a decision that the git history should have exactly one author who had actually read it.

### A hard split between model and math

The load-bearing design call of the whole application is that **the model decomposes and local code schedules**. The language model is asked for an ordered list of topics with effort estimates and is never asked for a date. A pure, exported, unit-tested function in `services/scheduler.ts` maps those topics onto real calendar days.

The specific algorithm matters more than the split alone. It maps each topic's *preceding* workload proportionally across the eligible days, which guarantees that the first topic lands on the first day and the last topic lands on the deadline, and which survives a topic that is individually larger than the daily budget. Greedy packing at a fixed daily budget does not: it front-loads, finishes weeks early whenever one topic overruns, and makes the deadline meaningless. The scheduler also refuses absurd windows outright rather than allocating a million date keys, treats a zero-minute topic as one minute so a degenerate estimate cannot collapse the spread, and special-cases a single topic to today rather than to the deadline. Because the calendar is arithmetic and not inference, deadline placement is exact, replanning is instant and free, and the entire feature works on a plane.

### Tests as the contract between sessions

With an agent doing much of the typing, invariants had to move somewhere they would survive any future conversation, and that place is the test suite. A completed slot is never deleted by reconciliation. A pending topic never receives a second live slot — a subtle one, because selecting purely on `assigned_date` double-books any topic that rollover has already carried somewhere else. A day with no goal-backed work answers the assistant's question **without calling the API at all**. A subtopic batch may only write its own topics, because without that filter a model that helpfully repeats earlier ids rewrites learning points the user has already read. Every backup table is asserted against the live schema, so a forgotten manifest entry cannot silently wipe data on restore. The React Flow pointer-events override is pinned by a test on three separate node types, because it is invisible in review and catastrophic in use. And the entire ffmpeg argument surface is asserted without ever starting a process.

The suite is 766 tests across 48 files, node-only — no DOM, no network, no process spawning — and the full run takes 4.45 seconds. That number is itself a design decision: a suite fast enough to run on every change is a suite there is never a reason to skip.

### Measure, don't vibe

When section-switching "felt laggy", the response was instrumentation rather than a guess. The packaged build was driven over the DevTools protocol against a *copy* of the real database, and the numbers came back clearly: click to board nodes in the DOM took 89 ms cold and 32 ms warm; long-task total across the switch was 0 ms on both runs; and the 280 ms transition itself ran at a median 16.7 ms frame gap — sixty frames a second, with one or two dropped frames.

So there was no stall. The transition *was* the delay. The real waste was somewhere else entirely: **28 IPC round trips per visit** to Storyboards, masked by zustand stores that survive unmount, so the remount painted instantly from cache while the mount effects silently re-fetched everything behind it — including one ffprobe spawn per take. Guarding those loads took a repeat visit to **zero** IPC calls.

The 280 ms transition was deliberately left alone, because shortening it would have made the application *feel* faster while being exactly as slow. An A/B test that removed every blur and backdrop-filter moved dropped frames from two to one, and from one to zero — noise — so the blur stayed as well. The profiler gets to overrule the hunch in both directions, and the second direction is the one that takes discipline.

## Architecture

### The trust boundary

The source tree is four directories, and the split between them is a security boundary rather than a filing convention. `src/main` is Node and privileged: SQLite, IPC handlers, all network access and all filesystem access live here. `src/preload` is the only bridge, exposing a single `window.deck` object through `contextBridge` and nothing else. `src/shared` holds types, date arithmetic, the IPC contract and the prompt registry, and is imported by both sides so that neither can drift from the other's idea of what a channel accepts. `src/renderer` is React with no `fs`, no database and no Node: every call it makes is an IPC round trip.

The boundary is enforced rather than merely intended. `contextIsolation` is on, `nodeIntegration` is off, and `sandbox` is true. Packaged builds receive a strict Content Security Policy whose `connect-src 'self'` is the load-bearing line — the renderer cannot open a socket even if a dependency wanted to, which is what makes "every provider call happens in main" a structural fact rather than a coding convention. The policy is applied to packaged builds only, because Vite's dev server needs inline scripts and a websocket for hot reload, and applying it in development would break the reload loop without making the shipped application any safer; development is covered by the renderer's own `<meta>` policy instead. Navigation guards bind on the app-level `web-contents-created` event rather than on the main window, so any future window is *born* guarded rather than remembered about: `will-navigate`, `setWindowOpenHandler` and `will-attach-webview` are all denied. Every URL that escapes to the operating system funnels through one function that gates to `https`, `http` and `mailto` — `shell.openExternal` with an unvetted string is the classic Electron hole, and here it exists in exactly one place. Browser permissions for camera, microphone, geolocation and page notifications are denied wholesale, because the application asks for none of them and therefore none should be grantable. And a single-instance lock prevents a second process from opening the same SQLite file behind the first one's back.

### `deck-media:` — the piece worth stealing

Storyboard reference media is linked, never copied. That is the right product decision — a director's reference folder is enormous and already organised — but it creates a genuine technical problem: `file://` will not load under the content security policy, and allowing `file:` would hand the renderer the entire disk.

The solution is that the renderer never learns a path at all. It asks for `deck-media://item/<id>`, and a privileged scheme handler in the main process does the lookup. There is no path in the URL to traverse, no `../` to sanitise, and the reachable set is exactly "media that some database row already points at" — rows which only ever get their paths from a file picker or a user's own drag, and which the IPC layer additionally refuses unless the extension is a known media type. The scheme is registered as `standard`, so the URL has normal origin semantics and is addressable from CSS and markup, and as `stream`, so a `<video>` element plays the response progressively instead of buffering the whole file first. Registration has to happen at module load, before `app.whenReady()`, with the handler itself installed afterwards once the database exists.

The same naming discipline governs the two classes of file the application *owns* rather than links. A generated clip is `<takeId>.mp4` and a generated character image is `<shotId>.png`; the id is a UUID, the path is derived from the id, and no database row stores a path at all. There is nothing to sanitise because there is nothing to traverse — a row that somehow named `../../etc/passwd` could not resolve to it, because the filename is computed from the id and never read from the row. Takes and portraits are deliberately separate modules rather than one generalised asset store, on the grounds that the two directories have different lifetimes, different sweeps and different extensions, and a shared abstraction would be one layer of indirection standing between a security property and the person reading it.

### Six ideas hold the data model together

**First, `slots` is the spine.** A manual task, a curriculum topic, a weekly recurring commitment and a project session are all rows in one table, distinguished by a `source` column. One table means one timeline, one ordering rule, and one definition of what "done" means. Partial unique indexes on `(source_ref, date)` make double-seeding structurally impossible rather than defensively checked.

**Second, rollover tombstones; it does not move.** When an unfinished slot on a past day is carried forward, the original is marked `rolled` and a *new* slot is created on today pointing back at it through `rolled_from`. Nothing is rewritten. This has three consequences that all matter: the miss stays visible in history, which is what keeps the Tracker honest; the renderer gets a stable pair of ids with which to run a shared-`layoutId` flight animation between cards, so the user watches the task move; and the operation is idempotent by construction, because only `open` rows on past dates are ever eligible, so re-running it within the same day is a no-op. Carried work is given a sort index below whatever today already holds, so it arrives at the top of the card rather than buried at the bottom.

**Third, the Tracker has no table.** Every number it shows is a query over slots that are already `done`. Nothing writes tracker data, which is what "automatically logged" ought to mean and rarely does. The one genuinely hard part is handled explicitly: `completed_at` is a UTC instant while the Tracker buckets by *local* day, so the SQL window is padded by a day on each side and the conversion is performed in JavaScript through the same local-time path the rest of the application uses, rather than delegated to SQLite's `localtime` modifier.

**Fourth, the model decomposes and local code schedules** — described above, and the reason reflow is instant, free and fully offline.

**Fifth, propose reads and asks; apply writes, in one transaction.** Every AI surface is split in two, and the split lives in the service rather than in the dialog. A plan the user rejects costs nothing but the wait, and one they partly accept is applied exactly as partly as they said. `services/video.ts` is the single deliberate exception and argues for itself in its own header: a generation job *is* durable state, because the provider's operation name must be on disk before the wait begins or quitting mid-generation loses a clip the provider has already been paid to produce. The exception is narrowed in writing — each write there is its own transaction, and none of them touch anything the user typed.

**Sixth, migrations are forward-only and append-only.** Fifteen of them, indexed by SQLite's `user_version`; a shipped entry is never edited and never reordered. They are kept as inline SQL rather than as `.sql` files so that the packaged bundle has no runtime asset resolution to get wrong inside an asar archive. The individual entries carry their own reasoning, and several carry lessons: v9 dropped a `CHECK` constraint on `projects.kind` on the explicit grounds that SQLite cannot alter a `CHECK` afterwards, so a third value later would mean rebuilding the table — a lesson subsequently cited by name in v15 for two further columns. v11 includes a data fix rather than only a schema change, rewriting every 5-second frame to 6 because the generator's real lengths are 4, 6, 8 and 10 and a row left at 5 would render with no length selected and then be rejected by the IPC validator on the next edit; it rounds up rather than to the arithmetic nearest, because 4 and 6 are equidistant and lengthening a shot someone already timed loses nothing where shortening it cuts a beat. v15 bundles two features in one entry, stating plainly that a `user_version` is per release and not per feature, and noting that nothing in it rebuilds a table: every column is an `ALTER` with a default, so a v14 database walks forward without a single existing value being touched.

### The contract, and the backup

The IPC surface is 21 namespaces and 162 registered handlers, defined once in `shared/ipc-contract.ts` and imported by both processes, so the renderer cannot invent a channel and the main process cannot silently change one's shape. Namespaces map to features rather than to tables — `days`, `slots`, `curriculum`, `notes`, `agent`, `tracker`, `stickers`, `tags`, `storyboards`, `takes`, `cut`, `styles`, `notify`, `settings`, `projects`, `bin`, `files`, `terminal`, `backup`, `system` — with `styles` separated out precisely because the style library is board-independent and a look outlives the film it was worked out on.

Backup is a JSON export and import of the whole database, table by table, and deliberately not a copy of the `.db` file. A file copy would be simpler but it is opaque: it cannot be read, diffed, or have one goal pulled out of it, and restoring one silently replaces a database the running process holds open in WAL mode. The tables are listed in dependency order so that inserts satisfy every foreign key without deferred constraint checking, and each ordering decision is commented where it is non-obvious. The version check is the part that actually matters: forward-only migrations mean an older backup is always safe to read, but a newer one may contain columns this build has never heard of, and importing it would drop them without a word — so a backup written by a newer build is refused outright. API keys are excluded by design, and a test asserts every backup table against the live schema so that a forgotten manifest entry cannot silently wipe data on restore.

Calendar arithmetic runs on `DateKey` strings rather than on `Date` objects carried across boundaries. Deciding *what today is* uses local time, but every key-to-key calculation is pure component math performed through `Date.UTC`, which makes `addDays` and `diffDays` exact across daylight-saving boundaries where naive local-midnight subtraction is off by an hour twice a year.

## The AI layer

### Two halves, cleanly split

`services/ai.ts` knows what Day Deck wants to ask a model — the prompts, the JSON schemas, the parsing, and the token budget each job is worth — and knows nothing about vendors. `services/providers/` knows how to put a request on the wire for a specific vendor and knows nothing about curricula or storyboards. That split is what lets the same curriculum breakdown run against Gemini, Grok, GPT or a local server without any of the prompt code learning that it happened. Each function in `ai.ts` also knows which key slot it belongs to, because that is a fixed property of the feature rather than something every caller should have to remember.

### A registry keyed by wire, not by brand

`PROVIDER_INFO` is the whole registry: nine providers — Gemini, OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, Groq, DeepSeek, Mistral, OpenRouter, and `custom` for anything OpenAI-compatible, which covers Ollama, LM Studio and a company gateway alike. Three text wires serve all nine, because most vendors ship an OpenAI-compatible endpoint and only Google and Anthropic speak genuinely different dialects. Adding a vendor that speaks an existing wire is one row in that table and zero lines of code.

Video is a second registry rather than a flavour of the first, and the reasoning is stated in the type itself: a text call is request in, JSON out, whereas every video API here is submit, then poll a long-running operation, then download bytes. `VideoAdapter` is its own interface, and `PROVIDER_INFO.videoWire` records which dialect a provider speaks or `null` if it makes no video at all. `null` is the honest answer for most of the table — OpenRouter, Groq, DeepSeek and the rest route *language* models, so a key that works perfectly for prompts cannot generate a clip however it is configured — and saying so in the registry is what lets the application explain that in a sentence and name the providers that *can*, instead of letting the call 404 about a missing model. Two dialects exist today: Google Veo through the Gemini API, and OpenAI's `/videos` resource, which `custom` also speaks, so a gateway or self-hosted server implementing the same resource works without a code change. `VideoAdapter.maxSamples` is stated rather than assumed, because Veo returns up to four samples from one operation where `/videos` produces exactly one per request, and a `sampleCount` sent to the second is silently ignored — the run simply comes back short.

Images are a third registry for the same class of reason: an image call is request in, **bytes** out, with no operation to follow but with a body neither of the other two shapes can express. The second image wire is the harder one, because OpenAI's `/images/generations` takes no reference at all and references require `/images/edits`, which is multipart where every other call in this application is JSON.

Each provider entry carries three separate default models — chat, video and image — and the second and third exist because of a real bug. A video slot nobody had configured once fell back to `defaultModel` and sent `gemini-3.5-flash` to a video endpoint, producing a 404 about a missing model on a button that looks like it should simply work; the only way to make it function was to know to open Settings and pick a Veo model by hand. The image default is a third field rather than a reuse of either, on the explicit grounds that reusing one would reproduce that bug exactly. Defaults are documented as fallbacks and not claims: the model dropdown is filled from each provider's own live list, so a default that ages out is a stale suggestion rather than a broken application, and an empty string means "there is no sensible guess — pick one".

### Seven key slots

Keys live in slots, one per feature: `default`, `goals`, `agent`, `scenes`, `prompts`, `video` and `image`. Each slot can independently name its own provider and model, and a slot with nothing of its own falls back to `default` — so one key in one slot runs the entire application, while a user who wants separate quotas can have them. The unit is per-feature rather than per-provider because per-feature is the unit people actually think in, and because a heavy storyboard session must not be able to starve the daily planner.

Two slots are separate for reasons worth stating. `video` is the only slot denominated in seconds of video rather than tokens, and the only one that can spend real money on a single click, so the key that writes prompts all day is deliberately not also the key that can be charged for a board's worth of generation. `image` is separate from `video` for the same reason the slots exist at all — they are two different bills. A portrait is a handful of cents and gets generated whenever somebody is describing a character; a clip is metered per second and gets generated deliberately. It is also the one slot that can legitimately name a different vendor from `video`: a Google video key alongside an OpenAI image key is a perfectly sensible arrangement, and slots are what make it expressible.

The fallback to `default` is **refused across vendors**, and that refusal is the single most valuable line in the provider layer. A key is only meaningful to the provider it came from; borrowing one produces an authentication failure whose message blames the key rather than the mismatch, which is the most confusing way this can possibly fail. When the provider-blind resolver was replaced, the old one was **deleted rather than deprecated**, so it cannot be reintroduced by accident.

Keys are encrypted through the OS keychain, one file per slot, and are read only inside the main process. There is deliberately **no IPC handler that returns a key** — the renderer can learn *whether* a slot is filled and nothing more, so a rogue script in the page has nothing to exfiltrate. Which provider a slot talks to is ordinary configuration, lives in the settings table, and belongs in a backup; only the secret itself is kept out of one, because the settings table is plaintext and the backup exporter dumps it verbatim, so a key that rode along in a backup file would outlive every promise the module makes. A single pre-slot key from an earlier version is renamed into `default` on first use, and the legacy filename is left saying `gemini` on the grounds that a file already on somebody's disk does not get renamed by hindsight.

### Failures that name the actual mistake

`services/providers/http.ts` is the one place this application opens a socket, and the one place that decides how a refused request should read. It classifies every failure into a small set of kinds — offline, auth, rate, credit, server, truncated, parse, config, unknown — and three of those get their own sentences, because each has a different fix and none of them is obvious from a status code.

A **rejected key** is the first. Gemini answers a bad key with a **400** saying "API key not valid", not a 401, so status alone would file the single most common failure in the application as a malformed request. Auth detection therefore sniffs the response body rather than trusting the code. The mirror-image problem appears at the other end of the range: Anthropic reports an overloaded model as **529** where Google says 503, and both mean busy rather than broken.

A **thin balance** is the second, and the most surprising. A credit-metered gateway reserves the *entire* output ceiling before running anything and refuses with 402 when the balance cannot cover the reservation — so a job whose real output was about 1,200 tokens was refused on a balance that covered 4,439, because it had asked for a 12,288-token ceiling. When the provider names an affordable figure in its refusal, the adapter re-asks for exactly that rather than failing. The lesson generalised into a design rule that now governs every limit in the prompt registry: **an output budget is a bill quoted up front, not a safety cap.**

An **overloaded model** is the third. The request was fine and the key was fine, so it gets its own longer, jittered retry budget rather than one sized for failures that do not heal on their own.

In front of all of that sits a proactive guard: a token bucket per key slot, in memory, refilling continuously rather than on a fixed window so that a burst is allowed and then calls flow at the configured rate with no cliff at the top of each minute. The retry logic in the adapters is reactive — it answers a 429 that has already happened — where the bucket ensures nothing in the application, including a renderer stuck in a loop, can fire more than the configured number of calls a minute per feature. Buckets are per slot for the same reason keys are. They are in memory on purpose: a restart refills them, which is the correct behaviour for a guard whose entire job is pacing a live process.

### The prompt desk

Ten AI processes exist in the application — `curriculum.decompose`, `curriculum.subdivide`, `day.agent`, `board.scenes`, `board.prompts`, `board.revision`, `board.cut`, `style.sort`, `cast.portrait` and `cast.pack` — and all of them are declared as data in `shared/processes.ts` rather than as prose buried in a builder function.

The shape of that registry is unusual, and the reason is stated first in the file. Every prompt in this application is *assembled*, not stored: the builder interleaves a fixed opening instruction, live data (the brief, the whole board, the cast, the placed style, the current batch) and a fixed rules list. You cannot hand a user "the prompt" as one editable string without either losing the data interleaving or inventing a template language — and a template language in Settings is a way to break the application from Settings. So the split is that **the parts that are prose become data, and the parts that are assembly stay code**. Each process declares a role sentence, a rules list, a key slot, a location label so a box in Settings can be found by feel, and a set of limits. Only the role and the rules are overridable; the JSON schema, the data blocks, and the parsing and clamping code are the contract between the prompt and the application and are not editable at all.

The validation around that is not defensive tidiness. An empty role falls back to the default, because an empty instruction is not a customisation but a broken call. Rules are trimmed and capped, and a **pinned** rule cannot be removed — only reworded. Editing *"Return each prompt with its scene number"* out of the list while the parser still keys on the scene number is a feature that breaks the application from Settings, so pinning makes that impossible while leaving the sentence's wording free, which is the part a user might actually want to change. Limits are clamped against bounds, because `output: 100` buys truncated JSON and a parse error with no clue as to why, and a `batch` of fifteen reproduces exactly the credit refusal that `PROMPT_BATCH = 4` exists to have fixed. The defaults are the exact text the builders used before the desk existed, moved out of `services/ai.ts` verbatim, which has two useful consequences: there is no behaviour change on first run, and the defaults are visible and resettable in Settings from day one.

## The seven rooms

### Today — the deck

The deck is the application's signature surface and its hardest piece of interaction engineering. Every card's scale, lift, blur, tilt and z-order derive from one continuous number: its distance from the centre in card widths. Because everything is a function of that single value, the whole stack stays glued to the finger instead of stepping between discrete states, and the peek treatment degrades smoothly rather than snapping at thresholds. The outermost card fades out entirely as it leaves, so cards do not pop out of existence at the edge of the mounted window.

Keyboard, flick and trackpad all route through one `commit()` function, which pre-offsets `dragX` by exactly one card width *before* the focus changes and then springs it back to zero. The cards' static positions jump by a card width when the offsets shift, and the pre-offset cancels that jump precisely — which is why a keypress, a drag release and a trackpad swipe produce identical motion rather than three subtly different animations. Any in-flight settle is stopped before a new one starts, so a fast second swipe cannot have the previous animation's completion clear state out from under it. Jumping more than a few days is treated as a jump rather than a swipe, because sliding through two hundred intermediate cards is just a blur.

The commit threshold is deliberately low — about a sixth of a card width, or a flick faster than 260 px/s — because past roughly a fifth of a card the user has unambiguously decided which way they are going, and making them drag a third of the way across is exactly what made the earlier version feel heavy: the gesture was over before the application agreed it had happened. The settle spring sits just on the critically-damped line at a high natural frequency, so there is no overshoot and the card is visually at rest in roughly half the time a softer spring took. The deck is the one surface in the application where settle time reads directly as lag, because the next swipe usually begins before the eye has stopped tracking the last one.

Quick-add parses what you type. `09:30 Review #amber PRs 45m` becomes a 45-minute slot starting at half past nine, filed under the amber tag, without the user touching a form field. The parse order matters and is commented: tags come out first, because a token sitting between the time and the duration would otherwise leave whitespace that the trailing duration match no longer sees. The parser lives outside its component so React Fast Refresh keeps working, since a module exporting both a component and a plain function loses it.

The rest of the room is built from the same spine. A timeline view lays the day out by hour. Reminder notes surface as a band on their date. Tags file work along a second dimension. The day-card assistant answers questions about the day. Today's card wears the application's one persistent "you are here": an animated amber border beam. Keyboard bindings are `←` and `→` to move days, `⌘T` to jump to today, and `Escape` to close panels, with the "is the user typing?" guard centralised in `lib/keyboard` so that every binding in the application shares one definition of it.

### The day assistant

The assistant on a day card is the smallest AI feature in the application and the most carefully fenced. Its entire design is the context it is handed: the model is never asked to plan, schedule or recommend — Goals Admin owns all three — only to explain what is already on a day. So the service's real job is assembling an exact, closed description of one day, and refusing to call out at all when there is nothing goal-backed on it to explain, which is asserted by a test.

Only the goals with work on *this* day are collected. A card that pulled in every active goal would let the assistant answer Tuesday's question with Thursday's material, which is the subtler version of the failure the feature exists to avoid. Curriculum text is excerpted rather than pasted whole, eight conversation turns are kept so that a follow-up like "and the second one?" resolves against what was actually said, and history is passed as real conversation turns rather than flattened into the prompt. The standing instruction that matters most is the last one: a model asked "what should I learn today?" with no boundary will happily invent a better curriculum than the one the user planned, which is worse than useless because it competes with the plan the whole application exists to protect. The given context is therefore the only thing it may answer from, and admitting a gap is an explicitly allowed answer.

### Goals Admin

Paste a curriculum and set a deadline, and every topic gets a date. The model returns an ordered topic list with effort estimates; the local scheduler assigns the days; the topics become `source='curriculum'` slots on the deck; and ticking one off on a day card marks the topic done. Topics are the source of truth and curriculum slots are a projection of them, which is what makes the projection safely rebuildable.

Reconciliation drops open slots whose topic has moved and creates whatever is missing, under two hard rules that are both pinned by tests: a completed slot is never deleted, and a pending topic never gets a second live slot. The second rule is the subtle one, because selecting purely on `assigned_date` double-books any topic that rollover has already carried to another day — which is why bootstrap re-points each topic at wherever its live slot actually is before anything else runs.

Reflow repacks everything still pending across the days that remain. Because it costs nothing and works offline, it always previews the exact date changes and waits for confirmation before rewriting a month of plans — a cheap operation is not an excuse to skip consent. When the remaining work genuinely no longer fits, reflow reports how many minutes a day it would actually take rather than quietly dropping topics. Several goals can run at once, each packing against its own daily budget so the scheduler stays pure per goal; the failure mode that creates is silent over-commitment across goals, and the overview surfaces that as a number against a global cap rather than enforcing a limit, because which goal to cut is not the application's call to make. Subtopics — the learning points a topic breaks down into — are generated in batches of fifteen, are display-only rather than individually checkable, and are reported rather than thrown on failure, because a breakdown is additive: a plan whose points failed to generate is a complete plan missing a nicety, not a failed plan. Reminder notes live in their own table rather than in `slots`, so "reminders never count as tasks" is structural rather than a filter that four separate readers each have to remember.

### Projects

Each project is a folder the user pointed at, so everything derived from it — the display name, the git branch, whether it still exists — is re-read rather than trusted from the row; the row is a cache that survives an unmounted volume, not the source of truth. Branch is read straight out of `.git/HEAD` rather than by shelling out, because `git` is not guaranteed to be on a GUI application's PATH and spawning a process per project on every list would cost far more than two small file reads; a folder that merely *looks* like a repository degrades to no branch instead of hanging on a subprocess.

Clicking a project card expands it into an embedded workspace: a Monaco editor, real pty-backed terminals, a file tree and git status, with an explicit "Open in VS Code" escape hatch. That pairing was an explicit decision over either half alone — the workspace is for staying in flow, and the escape hatch is for real work, where pretending to be an IDE would have failed at both. Monaco is roughly nine megabytes parsed, so it is a lazy chunk that the project grid warms on mount, and the code split sits *inside* the workspace rather than around it, because the shell has to mount immediately or the card's shared-element expand has no target to grow into.

Terminals are real shells owned by the main process; the renderer never spawns anything, it sends keystrokes and receives bytes. Every session is tied to the `webContents` that asked for it, so a renderer reload cannot orphan a shell holding a dev server open, and sessions are capped. Environment variables that describe the Electron process itself are stripped before spawning, because inheriting `ELECTRON_RUN_AS_NODE` turns the user's shell into a node process that exits immediately. Every filesystem read and write goes through one module that turns a project id and a *relative* path into a real location, resolving `..` before the prefix comparison so that `src/../../etc/passwd` fails the check rather than sneaking through, rejecting paths containing a NUL byte, refusing files over two megabytes, sniffing for binary content, and capping directory listings at a size that distinguishes a source tree from a build output. A symlink *inside* the project that points outside it is followed deliberately, on the stated grounds that real repositories use them and anyone who can plant one already has write access to the folder the user chose.

"Open in VS Code" is its own small study in not taking the obvious route. Shelling out to `code` fails on a normal machine, because the PATH shim is opt-in inside VS Code and a GUI application launched from Finder gets a minimal PATH regardless. So the launcher looks for the real binary at the paths it is actually installed at — across Code, Insiders and VSCodium — and falls back to `open -a`, which asks LaunchServices rather than PATH. The result is reported rather than guessed at.

Two further pieces round out the room. Projects can be `creative` rather than `code`, in which case they open an entry file such as a `.prproj` or `.aep` in its native application instead of the embedded workspace, and branch reads are skipped because a folder of footage is not a repository. And the desktop **bin** watches Desktop and Downloads with `fs.watch` and offers their contents for one-click filing into a project folder. The module only ever reads those directories; the actual move goes through the asset filer, and the one rule enforced at the boundary is the gate on what may be named as a source — a path under Desktop or Downloads and nothing else, so the renderer never gets to point the mover at `~/.ssh`. Dotfiles are skipped because the Desktop is full of files that are not the user's clutter, a vanished file between `readdir` and `stat` is skipped rather than crashed on, and a missing Downloads folder lists as empty. Folders in the panel map to real directories on disk, because without that mapping the panel would only be a second place for things to be lost while the files stayed on the desktop — which is the problem it exists to solve.

### Tracker

The Tracker is a diary you never have to keep, because it is a read rather than a record. It offers two segmentations of the same bars — by category or by tag — and never two charts, so that a day's height means the same thing either way and the shape of a week stays comparable across both views. Alongside the bars it reports pace against active goals, stays quiet when there are no goals rather than showing a meaningless zero, keeps every existing tag in the legend so the chart is stable across quiet weeks, gives untagged completed work its own segment rather than hiding it, and surfaces a weekly cutoff for any tag that has one.

### Stickers

A free-form board for the things that are not tasks, with drag physics, tilt, free-cell placement, board clamping, and optional folders. Deleting a folder releases its notes back onto the loose wall rather than shredding them. Temporary stickers whose day has passed are swept at launch, and the sweep *returns what it removed* rather than doing it quietly, so the renderer can fade them off the wall — a sticker disappearing is something the user watches happen rather than something they notice missing later. Deleting is preferred over marking them hidden, because a temporary sticker's whole contract is that it goes away, and a graveyard of them would make every later query carry a filter for rows nobody will ever look at again.

### Storyboards

This is the room that turned a planner into a studio, and it is the largest feature in the application by some distance.

A board is one film, laid out on a pan/zoom canvas. The brief is pinned at the origin and lives on the board row itself rather than in a nodes table — which is the whole trick, because there is no row to delete, so "always present, never deletable, exactly one per board" becomes a property of the schema instead of a rule the UI has to remember to enforce. Reference media hangs off the canvas in list nodes, wired either to the brief (where it informs every prompt) or to an individual scene (where it informs only that shot); both are things a director actually means, which is why film-wide-only was rejected. The one edge the graph actually stores is the reference wire, with real foreign keys and cascade on both ends, so deleting either node takes its wires with it and there is no orphan sweep to remember. Brief-to-scene and style-to-brief edges are deliberately absent because both are derivable — every frame already carries its board id, and a placed style always attaches to the one brief — and storing them would have meant a polymorphic edge table with no foreign keys at all, which is strictly worse than computing them.

**Planning happens in two stages.** Stage one breaks the brief into scenes; stage two writes the generation prompts. A single call that returned both was rejected, and the reason is that stage one inherits existing prompts positionally, so applying a scene breakdown **can never wipe a prompt** somebody has already refined. The scene-planning prompt asks a cinematographer to use the fewest continuous shots that can carry the brief, to cut only when the location, time, subject or framing must change, and never to cut for variety or pacing. The prompt-writing pass then writes each prompt to stand alone — never "as established" or "same style", because the model has not seen the other shots and cannot read the board — in one fixed order every time (subject, action, camera move, lens, lighting, environmental motion, grade, film stock, aspect), since a generator reads the front of a prompt hardest, so what is in frame goes first and how it was finished goes last. It names the camera move's speed and its start and end points, because a move with no end point generates as drift; it asks for one clear action plus one environmental motion, because three actions read as chaos; it requires motion to obey weight and contact; and it cuts adjectives that do not change the image, on the grounds that "cinematic" and "masterpiece" cost tokens and buy nothing. Prompts are written four scenes at a time, because fifteen at once reserved 12,288 output tokens up front and were refused on a balance covering about 4,400.

**Scenes stack into one container.** A board of twelve scenes is a list you read from top to bottom, not twelve rectangles to arrange, so they live in a single box wired to the brief, and the graph's spine collapses from N edges to one. A shut row shows its title, a line of description, and what the shot still needs — no prompt, prompt ready, N takes, or final; an open row is the full editor. Every row used to be that editor, which meant a box tall enough for two of them was presenting a list of twelve. The container sizes itself on shut rows up to a ceiling, past which the body scrolls. Each row carries its own target handle whose id is the frame id, open or shut, so a reference wire still lands on the scene it belongs to rather than on the container. A preference flips back to the free canvas, and nothing is rewritten by the switch — every frame keeps its coordinates either way.

**Looks are a reusable library.** Eighteen named styles ship with the application — MIDNIGHT LEXINGTON, WET NEON, PORTRA SUNDAY, FISHEYE VHS, SCOPE and the rest — each carrying both the verbatim source text and seven sorted fields: video style, camera, lens, grade, lighting, film stock and aspect. They seed through a migration rather than at startup, which is what makes a look the user deletes *stay* deleted, and each insert skips a name the library already holds so that a user's own version of a look survives. Styles are board-independent, because a look worked out once outlives the board it was worked out on. A style can be typed as free text and then sorted into the grid by the model, and "nothing invented" is enforced twice: once in the prompt, and once in code that drops any returned value none of whose words appear in the source text — a value that shares no word with the source cannot have come from it. The seven-field grid was kept over a text-first or free-text-only design because the grid is what makes a look reusable and is what the prompt builder assembles in a fixed order; a shot described as "35mm anamorphic" needs the lens to be a lens. In the most recent release the library was filed into eight families — anamorphic cinema, documentary handheld, lo-fi and analog, night and neon, editorial and portrait, macro and product, non-photoreal, and landscape and atmosphere — as a column rather than a table, because there is one category per style and nothing else hangs off a category, and uncategorised styles get their own trailing group rather than being hidden or silently filed under the first family. Dropping a style on a board is unique per board: the same style dropped twice is the same statement made twice, and the most recently placed style wins, because two styles on one board is a question with no good answer and asking the model to reconcile them would produce a look that is neither.

**The cast fixes who is in the film.** A style locks how the film is shot; a character sheet locks who is in it — the other half of shot-to-shot consistency, and the half a prompt cannot carry alone, because "the same woman as before" means nothing to a generator that has never seen the previous shot. A sheet starts as a description restated verbatim in every prompt. It then progresses through three states: `described`, `proposed` once portrait candidates exist, and `locked` once one is approved — and `locked` is the only state in which the image is attached to a generation. From the approved portrait, an angle pack of five views (front, three-quarter, profile, full body, expression) is generated *from that image* rather than re-prompted from the description, because re-prompting produces a second person who matches the description where passing the approved image in as a reference produces the *same* person from another angle. One pack image per view is enforced by a unique index, which is what makes "is the pack finished?" answerable by counting — and is why the pack needs no job row at all: the rows are the progress. Every candidate is kept rather than replaced, on the same principle that governs takes. Sheets are board-scoped rather than a global library, which was an explicitly decided question: a board is one film, a look is a house style worth reusing across films, and a cast belongs to one film.

**Generation keeps every take.** A scene's prompt produces a clip, and "regenerate" must never be the same gesture as "throw the last one away", because the previous take is often the better one and there is no undo for something that has to be paid for twice. Revision is done in words — type what you want changed and the prompt is rewritten for approval — and it applies through the same channel the prompt pass uses, so a revision has no write path of its own. Whether a scene is settled is a property of the scene (`is_final`), not of any one take, because a scene can be final with three takes on it and which of them is used is a separate question.

**The cut is a list of segments.** A timeline node holds `(take, start, end)` triples, which is what lets one scene contribute twice, or two of its takes back to back — neither of which a column on the take row could express. It can be populated from the scenes marked final, one full-length segment per final scene's chosen take in board order, deliberately not clever: it produces the obvious assembly, which is the thing you then trim. Because the stored duration is what was *asked* for and a generated clip's real length can differ, the files are probed when the timeline opens so the trim handles sit against the actual clip; a take that cannot be probed silently keeps its stored length, since that is very nearly right and refusing to show a timeline over it would be worse. Export is two ffmpeg passes rather than one filter graph — each segment trimmed to a temporary file, then the temporaries joined — because a single graph makes progress unreportable: ffmpeg would emit one time signal for the whole job and the user would watch a bar that moves once, at the end, on work that takes minutes. Finally, an AI trim pass watches the selected clips and proposes cut points with a reason for each, and writes nothing until accepted.

**Three guardrails exist because this is the part of the application that spends real money.** Only one video job may be in flight at a time, and that is checked against the database rather than against a variable in memory, so a job left running by a previous launch still counts. The clip count is stated before anything is submitted, because unlike a token budget this one is denominated in money that will appear on a bill. And jobs survive a quit: the provider's operation name is written down *before* the wait begins and re-polled at the next launch, because quitting mid-generation would otherwise lose a clip the provider has already been paid to produce. Polling runs every ten seconds with a ten-minute ceiling, chosen because Veo commonly takes one to three minutes and past ten something has gone wrong on the provider's side — and a timeout is not a lost clip, because the job row survives and the next launch re-polls the same operation. At launch the application also sweeps orphaned clip files, because cascade deletes remove take rows when a board goes and SQLite cannot delete files, so without the sweep deleting a board silently leaks every clip it generated — which for video is gigabytes nothing will ever mention again. None of the launch-time recovery steps is allowed to throw, on the grounds that a launch failing because last week's clip could not be tidied up is a far worse outcome than the clip staying untidy.

**ffmpeg** is `ffmpeg-static` only, and there is deliberately no ffprobe — a decision covered in the bug list below. Clips are re-encoded rather than stream-copied, because a trim lands on an arbitrary timestamp and `-c copy` can only cut on a keyframe, so a stream copy either moves the cut or leaves undecodable frames at the head of every segment, which plays back as a freeze. Joining uses the concat *filter* rather than the demuxer for the same class of reason: the demuxer is cheaper but produces frozen frames at boundaries unless every input shares a codec and timebase, which separately generated clips do not. The argument builders are pure and exported so that one test file asserts the entire decision surface without ever starting a process, which matters because a wrong flag here is not a crash — it is an export that succeeds and produces a film with frozen frames at every cut, discovered by watching the file.

### Settings

Settings is where the provider registry, the seven key slots with live model lists, the editable prompt desk, the theme, the notification briefs, the AI rate limit, launch-at-login and the JSON backup all live. Two of those deserve a note. The daily briefs are both **off** until asked for, because an application that pings you unprompted gets muted; they are driven by a one-minute poll rather than a computed `setTimeout`, because macOS suspends timers across sleep, so a laptop closed at 8am and opened at 11am would fire the morning brief three hours late, and the same timer would need re-arming on clock changes, daylight saving and time-zone moves — a minute tick re-reads the wall clock every time and cannot drift. A brief also has a ninety-minute grace window, past which the slot is claimed and skipped so that it cannot fire later: open the application at six in the evening and you want today's plan, not a "good morning". Launch-at-login is off by default and, when enabled, opens the window **visibly** — `openAsHidden` was the original choice and was wrong, because it produced a dock icon and nothing else, when seeing the day without asking for it is the entire point of the setting.

## The decision log, in prose

**Foundation, 2026-08-09.** Electron with Vite, React and TypeScript was chosen over Tauri, for the plain reason that node-pty, Monaco and better-sqlite3 all work without a Rust bridge. The embedded Monaco editor and real terminal were paired with an "Open in VS Code" button rather than either one alone, because the workspace exists for staying in flow while the escape hatch exists for real work, and pretending to be an IDE would have failed at both. A showcase, expressive animation tier was stated as a headline requirement rather than a nicety — the deck has to feel like shuffling physical cards, and subtle fades were explicitly rejected. And the model-decomposes/code-schedules split was set on day one, over the alternative of asking the model for dates, because models are unreliable at landing exactly on a deadline where arithmetic is not, and because the split is what makes reflow instant, free and offline.

**Goals and scheduling, 2026-08-09 to 2026-08-11.** A per-goal daily slice plus a global cap was chosen over one budget split by priority weight, so that each curriculum packs against its own minutes-per-day and the scheduler stays pure; over-commitment across goals is *reported* rather than vetoed. Reflow reports a shortfall in minutes per day rather than invoking an AI "re-scope" path that would rewrite topics to fit, because when the work genuinely does not fit the honest answer is the number and not a quietly shortened syllabus. AI subtopics are display-only rather than individually checkable, so that completion remains one yes/no on the parent task and the progress ring, the Tracker and rollover all keep their meaning. Reminder notes surface on a single date rather than following a recurrence rule, because "learned Saturday, review Wednesday" fires once and is dismissed. Those notes live in their own table rather than as a flag on slots, because everything that counts progress reads slots, so keeping notes out makes "reminders never count as tasks" structural. And voice input was deferred with a decision attached: when it is built it should call provider audio directly and reuse the key vault, with a bundled local Whisper as the later offline upgrade rather than the first implementation.

**Storyboards, the bin and keys, 2026-08-13.** The desktop bin was built as a watch-plus-one-click-approve flow using `fs.watch`, over both fully automatic file rules and a purely manual panel: automatic rules move files unseen, a manual panel is just Finder again, and the application carried exactly two runtime dependencies at the time, where chokidar would have been a third. One API key slot per feature with fallback to `default` beat both a single global key and per-provider keys, because per-feature is the unit people actually think in — and keys went into keychain-encrypted files rather than SQLite because the settings table is plaintext *and* is dumped verbatim by the backup exporter. The style tab kept its seven-field grid with a "sort into fields" button over both a text-first design and free text only. References wire to both the brief and to individual scenes rather than film-wide only. Planning was split into two stages rather than one call that returns both scenes and prompts. And auto-firing the model when a style is dropped onto a board was *removed* after being built, because everything AI is offer-then-approve and there are no exceptions that exist only because they are convenient.

**Multi-provider, 2026-08-14.** Adapters are keyed by wire rather than by brand, so three wires serve nine providers and adding a tenth that speaks chat-completions is one table row. Cross-vendor key borrowing is refused outright rather than falling back unconditionally, and the old provider-blind resolver was deleted rather than deprecated so it cannot come back. Video got its own registry and its own model fallback rather than being treated as a flavour of text generation. And `videoWire: null` is stated explicitly, with the resulting message naming the providers that *can* generate video, rather than letting a key that works perfectly for prompts fail with a 404 about a missing model.

**Light theme, 2026-08-14.** Colours live in Tailwind's `@theme` while shadows live in plain custom properties, because Tailwind v4 swaps colour tokens at runtime but bakes shadow literals in at build time — an elevation ramp declared in `@theme` silently never switches. Preferences live in the SQLite settings table rather than in localStorage, because `createWindow` has to read the theme to choose the window background *before* the renderer exists, which makes localStorage only a first-paint cache. And there is no inline `<script>` in `index.html`, despite that being the obvious no-flash trick, because the production CSP sends `script-src 'self'` and browsers enforce the intersection of all policies — it would have worked in development and broken in the packaged application.

**Styles and the cast, 2026-08-17 to 2026-08-18.** The eighteen shipped looks seed via a migration rather than at startup, so that a look the user deletes stays deleted while their own edited version survives. A character is locked by an approved portrait and its angle pack derives from that image, rather than by describing the character harder in every prompt, because words are necessary and not sufficient: two generations from identical words are two different faces. And the prompt desk exposes role and rules as editable text while schema and assembly stay code, rather than making "the prompt" one editable string, because every prompt here is assembled from interleaved live data — one editable string means either losing the interleaving or inventing a template language, and a template language in Settings is a way to break the application from Settings. Rules the parser depends on are pinned: rewordable, not removable.

**Money and safety, throughout.** Every take is kept rather than replaced by a regeneration. One video job may be in flight at a time, checked against the database rather than against a flag in memory. The clip count is stated before submission rather than shown as a progress bar after the fact. And `services/video.ts` is allowed to break the propose/apply rule, with the exception narrowed in writing: a job *is* durable state, so the operation name must reach disk before the wait begins, and each write there is its own transaction touching nothing the user typed.

## Ten bugs worth telling

**Every terminal spawn failed with a bare `posix_spawnp failed`.** pnpm's content-addressable store does not preserve file modes, so node-pty's `spawn-helper` arrived non-executable — and on macOS node-pty *execs* that helper rather than forking itself, so the failure surfaced as an opaque spawn error with nothing pointing at a permission bit. A postinstall script and an unpack rule fixed it, and the reflex it bought was worth more than the fix: packaged builds fail in ways development builds never show.

**Monaco 0.56 quietly restructured its ESM entry points**, turning the old deep import paths into doubled `./esm/vs/esm/vs/...` routes. Separately, the worker-backed language services are deliberately never imported at all: a packaged renderer loads from `file://`, where constructing a Worker is blocked, so the editor would have worked in development and broken in the shipped application. Four editor features that would reach for a worker on first use are disabled by hand.

**A pinned card whose textareas were all dead.** React Flow computes an internal `hasPointerEvents` flag from whether a node is selectable, draggable, or has click and hover handlers — and `onNodeDoubleClick` is not in that list — then stamps `pointer-events: none` on the wrapper when all of them are false. One style override fixed it, found the hard way; it is now pinned by tests on the brief node, the scene container and a third node type, each with a comment naming the trap the previous one already paid for.

**The deck swipe felt slow, and the spring was innocent.** Blur was re-enabled the instant a drag *ended*, so the entire settle animated four blurred layers at full repaint cost — and a trackpad swipe, which never sets a dragging flag at all, was blurred for its whole duration. A `moving` flag held until the settle actually completes fixed it. The related detail is that the blur is switched to `none` rather than to `blur(0px)`, because any non-`none` filter makes an element a backdrop root, and the focused card's glass would then have nothing behind it to sample.

**One flick advancing two days.** macOS momentum keeps firing wheel events for a second or more after the fingers lift, so a fixed unlock timer expires inside the same flick and the leftover momentum commits a second day. A latch tied to the gesture rather than the clock solves that, but then deliberate consecutive swipes feel broken, because the second one lands inside the first one's tail and vanishes. The naive fix — treat a magnitude spike as a new gesture — double-advances on a hard flick, because the commit fires while the fingers are still *accelerating* and deltas keep rising afterwards. Momentum, by contrast, only ever decays. So a new swipe is recognised only once a delta has been seen falling and then jumps back up.

**The light theme that half-shipped.** Tailwind v4 swaps colour tokens at runtime but bakes shadow literals in at build time, so the elevation ramp declared in `@theme` silently never switched. It was verified by grepping the built CSS, which became the house rule for any claim of that shape. Monaco and xterm compounded it by holding their themes imperatively: both need a fresh theme object, and xterm additionally needs its WebGL texture atlas cleared, or scrollback keeps repainting in the old palette.

**A bad API key that reports as a malformed request,** and a gateway that reserves your entire output ceiling before running anything. Two completely different failures that both present as "the AI is broken" and have completely different fixes — one requires sniffing a 400 body rather than trusting the status code, and the other required re-asking for the figure the provider itself named as affordable.

**The database that lied when copied.** A live SQLite file in WAL mode, copied plainly, yields the last *checkpoint* and silently omits recent writes. It cost a wrong conclusion about which styles existed in the library. The rule that came out of it is `.backup` or nothing.

**An ffprobe that cannot run on the machine it shipped for.** `ffprobe-static@3.1.0` places an x86_64 binary inside its `darwin/arm64` directory, so on Apple Silicon without Rosetta it cannot run at all — and it fails as `bad CPU type in executable`, which reads like a corrupt download rather than a package shipping the wrong file. Durations now come from `ffmpeg -i` instead: the same information, one binary, and one that is by construction the same architecture as the encoder. The related packaging trap is that an executable cannot run from inside an asar archive at all, so ffmpeg is unpacked and the reported path is rewritten from `app.asar` to `app.asar.unpacked` — the same fix node-pty's spawn-helper had already demanded.

**An export that succeeds and produces a broken film.** A trim lands on an arbitrary timestamp while `-c copy` can only cut on a keyframe, so a stream copy either moves the cut or leaves undecodable frames at the head of every segment, which plays back as a freeze. Joining with the concat demuxer fails the same way at boundaries unless every input shares a codec and timebase, which separately generated clips do not. This is precisely why the argument builders are pure and exported and covered by tests: a wrong flag here is not a crash, it is a file you only discover is broken by watching it.

## Evidence

Every figure below was re-verified against the repository rather than recalled. The test suite is **766 tests across 48 files, all passing in 4.45 seconds** — node-only, with no DOM, no network and no process spawning. The history is **43 commits** across three feature branches with conventional-commit hygiene throughout, totalling 249 files and 66,210 insertions from the initial commit to HEAD. The schema is **15 forward-only migrations**, with a test that walks a v1 database all the way forward. The process boundary is **21 IPC namespaces and 162 registered handlers**, shared by both processes from one contract file. The source tree is **178 TypeScript files, about 46,000 lines**. The performance receipts are 60 fps deck interaction, section mount at 89 ms cold and 32 ms warm, 0 ms of long tasks across a section switch, and redundant per-visit IPC reduced from 28 calls to zero.

Verified end to end: the full suite, both typechecks, a packaged `dist:mac` arm64 build, the v11-to-v13 migration against a real database, the render pipeline against real media through the packaged application's own bundled binary, and the entire generation path — submit, poll, download, take row — against a local server speaking the `/videos` dialect.

**What is not verified, stated plainly.** Veo and Sora generation and the AI trim have not been run against a live vendor; they require a billing-enabled key in the `video` slot, and the protocol was exercised against a local stub instead. The build is unsigned and unnotarised. The application is single-machine by design, so there is no sync to test. And there are no usage metrics at all, because a local-first application with no telemetry is a decision rather than an oversight.

## What is still open

The four changes planned as `frosted-doughnut` all shipped — the locked cast, the filed style families, the editable prompt desk, and the lag work. What remains open is smaller and more honest. Live-vendor verification of Veo and Sora generation and of the AI trim is the first item: the protocol is proven against a stub, the vendors are not. Code signing and notarisation are the second; the DMG is unsigned today. Per-style negative prompts are the third: the field exists in the video adapter and is read by the Veo wire, but nothing populates it, and style extras are not a substitute because they render into the *positive* prompt where a negative actively harms the generation — wiring it is a provider-path change rather than a UI one. Voice capture remains deferred, with the decision about how to build it already made. The re-scope path for when remaining work genuinely cannot fit is deliberately unbuilt, because reflow reports the shortfall in minutes per day and it is not yet clear that an AI rewrite of the syllabus is better than that number. And sync stays out of scope by design.

## Reflection

Vibe-coding at this scale worked because the vibes were fenced. The agent supplied speed and breadth; the human supplied taste, priorities and veto power. The artifacts that made the collaboration durable were unglamorous ones: plan documents written before any code, decisions recorded together with the alternative that lost, invariants pushed into a test suite fast enough that skipping it was never tempting, file headers that argue with the reader about why a module refuses to do the obvious thing, and a habit of measuring before optimising — including twice accepting a measurement that said the optimisation was not worth doing.

The most transferable insight turned out to be the product's own design language rather than anything about the tooling. **AI proposes, you approve** is a rule that turns generative systems from slot machines into instruments: it makes a wrong answer cheap, it makes a partly-right answer usable, and it keeps the person holding the pen. It is also, not coincidentally, exactly how this application was built.

*Built solo, conversationally, in public view of its own git history.*
